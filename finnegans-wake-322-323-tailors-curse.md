# The tailor's curse: *Finnegans Wake* 322.35–323.24

A line-by-line explication of the "culp me goose" paragraph (Book II, chapter 3). It was built from seven research passes run in parallel: four close readings, one per quarter of the paragraph, plus separate passes on context and drafts, on languages, and on themes. The results were checked against the standard annotations (McHugh, Glasheen, Christiani, O Hehir, Bonheim, Mink), *A Wake Newslitter*, the James Joyce Digital Archive's draft transcriptions, and *Genetic Joyce Studies*.

---

## In one paragraph

Kersse the tailor is losing his temper. A hunchbacked Norwegian sea captain ordered a suit from him and sailed off without paying. When he came back, he found the suit didn't fit and blamed the tailor's needlework. This is the tailor's reply.

He calls the captain every name he can find: pirate, Viking, Flying Dutchman, bugger, skirt-chaser, thief, welsher, turncoat, disgrace to his church. He curses him and threatens to spit in his face and kick his backside. Then he lands the punchline he has been building to: there isn't a tailor in all Ireland or Scandinavia who could make a coat and trousers for a foreigner with "that hell of a hull of a hill of a camelump" on his back. Fit him? *Fadgestfudgist!*

Joyce took the story from his father's repertoire of party pieces. In the book it is told in HCE's pub, where the drinkers use it to needle the publican, himself a hunchbacked, foreign-descended "invader". So the curse is also a native cursing an outsider and a son cursing his father. It is one more replay of the *Wake*'s central event, the fall of the hump-backed father.

---

## 1. Where we are

**Book II, chapter 3.** It is night in Humphrey Chimpden Earwicker's pub in Chapelizod, on the Liffey just west of Dublin. The customers drink while stories come at them, partly told by the drinkers and partly over the radio.

**The yarn (FW 311–332).** It is announced 300 pages earlier as "How kirssy the tiler made a sweet unclose to the Narwhealian captol" (23.10–11). In outline:

1. A Norwegian captain asks a "ship's husband" (a ship's agent ashore) where to get a suit made. He is sent to Kersse the tailor and measured. Then he sails off for seven years without paying, to cries of "Stolp, tief, stolp, come bag to Moy Eireann!" ("Stop, thief, stop, come back to my Erin!").
2. He returns, and the suit doesn't fit.
3. At **320.01–17** the captain curses the tailor: "that woe worstered wastended shootmaker whatever poked a noodle in a clouth!"
4. On **p. 322** the pub crowd jeers at Kersse: "you scum of a botch … sew wrong, welsher".
5. **322.35–323.24, this paragraph:** the tailor's counter-curse.
6. At **323.25** radio static and thunder cut in: "Upon this dry call of selenium cell … doomed crack of the old damn ukonnen power". HCE, "the lord of the saloom", comes back to his customers and senses "their joke was coming home to them".
7. The ship's husband makes peace and plays matchmaker. The captain is baptised a Catholic ("I popetithes thee, Ocean") and married to a local girl, in most readings the tailor's own daughter. He settles down: "his loudship was converted to a landshop".

**The family joke behind it.** Philip McCann, Joyce's godfather and a ship's chandler, told John Joyce about a hunchbacked Norwegian captain who ordered a suit from J. H. Kerse, a tailor at 34 Upper Sackville Street. In Ellmann's words: "The finished suit did not fit him, and the captain berated the tailor for being unable to sew, whereupon the irate tailor denounced him for being impossible to fit."

John Joyce made it a party piece. Joyce said that if *Finnegans Wake* ever reached his father in the afterworld, his verdict would be: "Well, he can't tell that story as I used to and that's one sure five!"

The captain's "unable to sew" is on page 320. **This paragraph is the "impossible to fit".**

## 2. Who is speaking

The yarn tags its speakers by how they spell "said". Roland McHugh worked this out in *A Wake Newslitter* (1968), and the drafts show Joyce building the system in revision.

| Spelling | Speaker |
|---|---|
| *sagd* (Danish *sagde*) | the captain |
| **sazd** | **Kersse the tailor** (8 times here) |
| *sayd* | the ship's husband |
| *szed* | "the head marines talebearer" (325.17); once here |

So "he sazd, szed the ham…" means "he [the tailor] said, says the tale-bearer". We hear the tirade second-hand, relayed by a pub gossip or broadcaster, the way all news travels in the *Wake*. The refrain "he sazd" gives it the rhythm of an angry man being quoted: *says he … says he … says he*.

## 3. How Joyce built it

The 1936 first draft is a single sentence:

> …and so culp me goose, he said, the bugganeering wanderducken (that his pumps may ship awhole shandymound of the dussard), there is never a tailorman in the Fife Folks of Iseland from Drumadunderry till the rumnants of Mecckrass, could milk a colt in thrushes foran furrow follower width that camelump back.

Roughly: *"So help me God," he said, "that buccaneering Flying Dutchman (may desert sand choke his pumps!): there isn't a tailor in Ireland, from Derry to Kerry, could make a coat and trousers for a foreign fellow with that camel-hump back."*

That sentence is the spine of the paragraph. Over the next two years Joyce packed everything else in between the oath and the punchline. The dates come from the James Joyce Digital Archive's draft transcriptions.

**November 1936**
- The tale-bearer: "the ham muncipated … recoursing".
- The collars-and-cuffs dandy.
- The Viking kings and the language-invasion clause.
- "the kurss of all portnoysers".
- The flags and "Donnerbruch".
- The pulp / hulp / yulp sentence.
- "pushkalsson", the bellows pockets and the Russian church.
- "said" becomes "sasd".

**December 1936** (for the magazine *transition*, 1937)
- "sazd" and "szed".
- "there's nice tugs he looks (how you was, Ship Alouset?)".
- "voyaging after maidens, belly jonah…" and "one to one".
- The whole block from "Reefer was a wenchman" to "a salestrimmer!".
- The potatoes and the fox; "drop down dead and deaf".
- "tailorman" becomes "teilwrmans"; "Skunkinabory" is added; "back" becomes "bakk".

**December 1937**
- "Fadgestfudgist!"

**1938 galleys**
- "a hole in his tale and that hell of a hull of a hill of a", taken from a notebook jotting, "with the hill of a back".

Two sources for these additions have been traced through Joyce's notebooks, both reported in *Genetic Joyce Studies*:

- **The Viking names** come from J. J. A. Worsaae, *An Account of the Danes and Norwegians in England, Scotland, and Ireland* (1852): the kings Erik Blodöxe and Harald Blaatand, Harald Hardrada's banner "Landöde" ("the land-ravager"), and the Danish royal flag, the Danebrog.
- **The Welsh-English idiom** comes from Caradoc Evans's play *Taffy* (1923). These lines from it all surface here, bent:
  - "What for that tape measure, shall I ask?"
  - "there's healthy you look"
  - "How you was, Marged fach?"
  - "if I was forty years ago"
  - "I will speak one to one now"
  - "Three kicks the boy will have"
  - "patch-tato"
  - "drop dead and blind"

**Text note.** Every draft up to the 1938 page proofs reads "**kurss**" (curse, and also the tailor's name) where the 1939 first edition prints "hurss". The change is probably a misprint.

## 4. The paragraph in plain English

> "So help me God," he said (so the story goes, as told by some tavern Ham going over the old course again, all choler and coughing). "Look at him in his collars and cuffs, a Beau Brummell out on the spree, the buggering buccaneer of a Flying Dutchman (may his pumps fill up with the sand of the desert!), that coarse-haired corsair, that highwayman of the high seas. Oh, doesn't he look fine in his togs! (How are you, Chapelizod?) That bloody Viking, Eric Bloodaxe and Harald Bluetooth in one pirate ship, who's sneaking into our language and our navy by the back passage, God confound him, chasing the girls on every voyage, a Jonah after the women! The curse of all the tailors on him! I'll spit in his face, man to man, the land-ravager, like they do at Donnybrook Fair.
>
> "Taffy was a Welshman, Taffy was a thief, and this one's a sailor and a wencher. You can smell on his wet clothes that he's come from some beach where he broke his promise to a girl. Where's that old lecher got to now, may I ask? He'd get three kicks up the backside from me, the turncoat, if I were a few years younger. *Mister* Captain Goose-Cooker, if you please, sail-trimmer and time-server! He was pawing my leather like it was pulp, and I was slaving over his frills like a skivvy. He'll feel the weight of my fist, and he'll yelp for help!
>
> "That gorbellied, mountain-humped son-of-a-gun Humpson, with his baggy pockets stuffed with spuds and a wolf in his belly, a disgrace to his Roman Catholic church, may he drop down dead! There isn't a tailor in the five provinces of Ireland, or in the whole length of Scandinavia, from Derry to the ruins of Muckross, could make a coat and trousers for a foreigner with that hole in his backside and that hell of a hump on his back. Fit him? Fudge!"

## 5. Line by line

### 1. "And so culp me goose,"

**Plain sense:** "And so help me God!", an oath. In print it opens with a speech dash.

- **culp** = Latin *culpa*, "fault", as in *mea culpa*. The oath is also a confession, but turned outward: *his* fault, not mine.
  - The *Wake*'s great refrain is *O felix culpa*, "O happy fault", the Fall that led to redemption. It appears as "O foenix culprit!" at 23.16.
- **goose:**
  - "To cook someone's goose" means to ruin them.
  - A *tailor's goose* is the heavy smoothing iron with a goose-neck handle (McHugh). The tailor swears by his own iron.
  - The goose comes back as "Gaascooker" (Danish/Norwegian *gaas*, "goose").
- **"And so"** continues the line before. There the drinkers end a mock-litany with "libitate nos, Domnial!": *libera nos, Domine*, "deliver us, O Lord", plus Daniel O'Connell, the Liberator. The prayer turns into an oath.

### 2. "he sazd, szed the ham muncipated of the first course, recoursing,"

**Plain sense:** "he said, says the tale-bearer, going back over it all again".

- **sazd / szed:** the tailor's tag, then the relayer's (see §2).
- **ham muncipated = the Emancipator**, Daniel O'Connell (McHugh; Tindall). He answers the "Domnial" of the line before: O'Connell won Catholic Emancipation in 1829.
  - "Muncipated" also hides **municipal**: O'Connell became Lord Mayor of Dublin in 1841.
  - It also holds Irish ***mún***, "urine" (O Hehir), which sets up "pumps" two lines later.
- **Ham** is a probable further layer; McHugh glosses only O'Connell. Noah's son Ham saw his drunken father naked, told his brothers, and brought down Noah's curse (Genesis 9). He is the son who exposes the father. That is what this gossip, and this tailor, are doing to the captain, who stands for HCE, the father.
  - The *Wake* elsewhere ties Noah to the hump and the camel: "Sire Noeh Guinnass, exposant of his bargeness … to hump the body of the camell" (549.34–35).
  - "Ham" is also the meat course and a ham actor.
- **Who is "the ham"?** In Joyce's first typescripts this phrase was one more insult aimed at the captain. When he put "szed" in front of it (December 1936), it became the speaker. Both readings survive.
- **first course, recoursing:**
  - Vico's *corso* and *ricorso*, history's cycle coming round again (McHugh). This is the *Wake*'s structural principle.
  - **Re-cursing.** The tailor's name, Kersse, is "curse". Just before this, the text spells "course" as "koursse", where Kersse "stood them the whole koursse" of a race meeting.
  - The first course of a meal, which "dussard" (dessert) closes three lines later.

### 3. "all cholers and coughs with his beauw on the bummell,"

**Plain sense:** spluttering with rage; and also all collars and cuffs, a dandy on a spree.

- **cholers and coughs:**
  - "Collars and Cuffs" was the nickname of Prince Albert Victor, Duke of Clarence, known for his high collars and long cuffs (McHugh; Glasheen). This is pure dandy-and-tailor language.
  - **Choler** (rage) plus coughing. The grammar lets it describe either the furious teller or the over-dressed captain.
- **beauw on the bummell:**
  - **Beau Brummell**, the Regency dandy and dictator of men's tailoring. He fled to France in 1816 to escape his creditors, like a captain skipping his tailor's bill.
  - German ***Bummel***, "a stroll, a spree", as in Jerome K. Jerome's *Three Men on the Bummel*; also **bum**.
  - **Beau** = suitor; **bow** = a ship's bow, or a stoop (the hump). The draft read "bow".
  - Norwegian *bomull* = "cotton" (Tysdahl & Hart).

### 4. "the bugganeering wanderducken, he sazd,"

**Plain sense:** that buccaneering Flying Dutchman.

- **bugganeering** = buccaneering + **bugger**.
- **wanderducken = Vanderdecken**, captain of the Flying Dutchman, doomed to sail forever.
  - In Wagner's *Der fliegende Holländer* he may land once every seven years to seek a faithful woman. He meets a **Norwegian captain**, Daland, and is betrothed to Daland's daughter Senta.
  - Those are this yarn's ingredients, reshuffled: a cursed wanderer, seven-year voyages, a Norwegian sea captain, and a marriage arranged ashore. Timothy Martin (*Joyce and Wagner*) shows Joyce working the opera into the whole episode.
- Joyce had used the phrase before. In *Ulysses* ("Circe"), Bloom as Lord Mayor denounces "our bucaneering Vanderdeckens in their phantom ship of finance" and "these flying Dutchmen or lying Dutchmen". John Gordon reads this whole tirade as modelled on that xenophobic speech.
- German ***wandern*** = "wander"; ***ducken*** = "stoop, duck". So also the stooping hunchback who ducked out on his bill.

### 5. "(that his pumps may ship awhoyle shandymound of the dussard),"

**Plain sense:** an Irish-style curse, "may his pumps take in a whole sand-hill of the desert!" (McHugh: "may desert sand block his pumps!").

- **pumps … ship:**
  - A ship "ships" water when waves come aboard, so pumps that ship sand are finished.
  - "To pump ship" is sailors' slang for urinating (McHugh), so it also means *may he never piss again*. Tindall has the captain stepping out to do just that.
  - Pumps are also dancing shoes, and a pub's beer pumps.
- **awhoyle** = "a whole" + "ahoy!" (McHugh), in a stage-Irish "awhile" accent.
- **shandymound:**
  - **Sandymount**, the Dublin strand where Stephen walks in "Proteus" and Bloom ogles Gerty in "Nausicaa".
  - Sterne's *Tristram Shandy*.
  - Shandy, the pub drink.
  - Dialect *shandy* = "half-crazy".
- **dussard** = desert (the draft has "dusert") + **dessert**, which closes the meal begun at "first course".
- **"Ship of the desert"** = the camel (McHugh). The hump that ends the paragraph is already being loaded.

### 6. "the coarsehair highsaydighsayman,"

**Plain sense:** the coarse-haired corsair, the highwayman of the high seas.

- **coarsehair:**
  - **Corsair**, a pirate, as in Byron's *The Corsair*.
  - **Harald Fairhair**, Norway's first king, made coarse (Gleckner). His son, Eric Bloodaxe, arrives two phrases later.
  - The shaggy sea-dog; the coarse hair-canvas tailors put inside coat fronts.
- **highsaydighsayman:**
  - **Highwayman** + **high seas** (McHugh).
  - "Sayman" is "seaman" in a Dublin mouth ("tay" for "tea").
  - There is a hey-diddle jingle in it too.

### 7. "there's nice tugs he looks, (how you was, Ship Alouset?) he sazd,"

**Plain sense:** "Oh, doesn't he look fine in his togs! (How are you, Chapelizod?)"

- **there's nice … he looks** is Welsh-English ("there's lovely you look"), taken from Caradoc Evans's *Taffy*, where it is "there's healthy you look". It is pure sarcasm: look at him swanking in the suit he says doesn't fit.
- **tugs** = togs (clothes) + tugboats.
- **how you was** is pidgin, also lifted from *Taffy*: "How you was, Marged fach?"
- **Ship Alouset = Chapelizod** (O Hehir; McHugh). Its Irish name, *Séipéal Iosaid* ("Iseult's Chapel"), sounds like "Shapel Isod".
  - Chapelizod is the village of HCE's pub, so the aside is addressed to the very house the story is being told in. It sounds like a broadcaster's "Hello, Chapelizod!", or the tailor mimicking the captain hailing the locals.
  - Swapping "Ship" for "Chapel" turns the pub into a ship.
  - Iseult points to Issy, the daughter the captain is "voyaging after".

### 8. "the bloedaxe bloodooth baltxebec,"

**Plain sense:** that bloody Viking pirate-ship of a man.

- **bloedaxe:** **Eric Bloodaxe**, son of Harald Fairhair, later king of York. Dutch *bloed* = "blood". Eric is said to have earned his nickname by killing his brothers, which fits a book built on warring brothers.
- **bloodooth:** **Harald Bluetooth**, king of Denmark; both names come from Worsaae. Also:
  - **blood-oath**, the blood-brotherhood oath of Wagner's *Götterdämmerung* (Martin; Hodgart & Bauerle). The curse looks ahead to the reconciliation two pages later, when sailor and tailor become "Brothers Boathes, brothers Coathes, ye have swallen blooders' oathes" (325.25–26).
  - Dutch *blood*, "timid, cowardly" (Knuth), so a bloodthirsty Viking who is also a coward.
- **baltxebec:** **Baltic** + **xebec** + **Beelzebub**. A xebec is the small three-masted Mediterranean ship of the Barbary corsairs (McHugh).

### 9. "that is crupping into our raw lenguage navel through the lumbsmall of his hawsehole, he sazd,"

**Plain sense:** who's sneaking into our language and our navy through the back passage.

- **crupping:** creeping (Norwegian *krype*) + **crupper**, the strap that runs under a horse's tail.
- **raw lenguage navel:**
  - "royal English navy" (McHugh);
  - "our language" (Spanish *lengua*, "tongue");
  - naval / **navel**, the front of the body, as the hump is the back.
- **lumbsmall:**
  - The **small of the back** (Latin *lumbus*, "loin"; also "lump").
  - Norwegian ***Landsmaal***, the 19th-century movement for a native vernacular Norwegian. Christiani argues Joyce pairs it with Irish Gaelic, and Riksmaal (Dano-Norwegian) with English.
  - Norwegian *maal* also means **measure**: the tailor's tape.
- **hawsehole:**
  - The hole in a ship's bow for the anchor cable, plus **arsehole**.
  - In naval slang, to "creep in through the hawsehole" is to be promoted from the lower deck, a jumped-up rating (McHugh).
- **The whole clause:** an upstart foreigner has wormed his way into our language and our navy by the back door, with a buggery joke carried over from "bugganeering".
- It is also a historical joke. Irish took much of its seafaring and trading vocabulary from the Norse, and Dublin itself was a Viking foundation.
- **The accuser is guilty of the same thing.** Kersse has just come back into the yarn in a sailor's rig, looking "a novicer on the nevay" (322.04). Campbell & Robinson: "Kersse has joined the English navy". He accuses the captain of what he has just done himself.

### 10. "donconfounder him, voyaging after maidens, belly jonah hunting the polly joans,"

**Plain sense:** God confound him, always chasing the girls, the Jonah of a womaniser.

- **donconfounder:**
  - "God confound him" + **dumbfound** (McHugh) + **founder** (a ship going down) + a Spanish **Don**.
  - Dutch *donder* and Norwegian *dunder* both mean "thunder".
  - One breath after "language", "confound" also calls up Babel, where God says "let us … confound their language" (Genesis 11). [My reading.]
- **voyaging after maidens:**
  - A "maiden voyage" turned around.
  - The Dutchman's endless search for a faithful maiden; the sailor with a girl in every port.
- **belly jonah:** **Jonah**, who fled God by ship and ended up in the whale's belly. Sailors call a bad-luck shipmate "a Jonah". "Belly" points ahead to the "gorbellied" captain.
- **polly joans:** no standard gloss. Most likely just girls, any Polly or Joan, with "Joan" chiming against "Jonah". Other possibilities:
  - Davy Jones: the yarn already has "Divy and Jorum's locquor", Davy Jones's locker (316.19).
  - [My guess] The "Paul Jones", the 1920s–30s ballroom mixer in which partners keep changing.
  - [My guess] John Paul Jones, the American captain whom the British called a pirate and who raided the Irish Sea in 1778.
- **Taken together** (Martin): "'voyaging after maidens' and 'belly jonah hunting the polly joans' describe the Dutchman's situation perfectly".

### 11. "and the hurss of all portnoysers befaddle him, he sazd,"

**Plain sense:** "and the curse of all tailors befall him!"

- **portnoysers:** Russian ***portnoi*** = "tailor" (Škrabánek; McHugh). Leeming describes the tailor "in a towering polyglottal rage" calling down "the curse of all tailors". Also "port-noisers": sailors raising hell in port.
- **hurss:** "curse".
  - The drafts read **kurss**, so it is curse + Kersse: the tailor's very name is a curse.
  - The 1939 *h* looks like a misprint, though "horse", "hearse" and "whores" have all been heard in it.
- **befaddle:** befall + befuddle + fiddle-faddle; German *befallen*, "to strike, afflict".
- **The curse in the Dutchman plot.** Martin: the tailor's curse "makes him the counterpart of the devil" who cursed the Dutchman to wander.
- **A tailor's curse** was also an English idiom for something worthless ("don't care a tailor's curse"). So the whole tirade is proverbially worth nothing.

### 12. "till I split in his flags, he sazd, one to one, the landslewder, after Donnerbruch fire."

**Plain sense:** "till I spit in his face, man to man, the land-ravager, the way they do at Donnybrook Fair!"

- **split in his flags:**
  - **spit in his face**;
  - **split**, as a tailor rips a seam;
  - flags = a ship's colours.
  - It grows out of Joyce's notes from Worsaae on the Danebrog, the Danish flag; Danish *splitflag* is its swallow-tailed form. Tearing the enemy's flag is an act of war.
  - It reverses the start of the yarn. There the deal was sealed by spitting in the fist, "He spit in his faist (beggin)" (311.31). The spit that made the contract becomes the spit of insult.
- **one to one** = "man to man", single combat, from *Taffy*: "I will speak one to one now". It joins the chapter's chain of tit-for-tat formulas: "tig for tager … strop for stripe", "gig for gag", "fag for fig" (p. 322).
- **landslewder:**
  - Harald Hardrada's raven banner **Landöde**, "the land-ravager" (from Worsaae);
  - landlubber; landslide (a fall); lewd;
  - Norwegian/Danish *sludder*, "drivel";
  - German *Landsleute*, "fellow countrymen".
- **Donnerbruch fire:**
  - **Donnybrook Fair**, Dublin's annual fair, so notorious for fighting that "a donnybrook" means a brawl.
  - German ***Donner*** ("thunder") + ***Bruch*** ("break", as in *Wolkenbruch*, "cloudburst"): a thunderclap. In the *Wake* thunder always means Vico's thunder and the Fall.
  - The **Dannebrog** again. Legend says the Danish flag fell from the sky during a battle in 1219.
  - "After the fair" also means too late.
- **The tags stop here.** The next five sentences come without "he sazd", as if the tailor's voice has taken over the telling.

### 13. "Reefer was a wenchman."

**Plain sense:** "Taffy was a Welshman, Taffy was a thief…"

- **The nursery rhyme** (Hodgart & Worthington; McHugh) goes on: "Taffy came to my house and stole a piece of beef."
  - The suppressed "was a thief" is the point: the captain took the suit and never paid.
  - Earlier the crowd called Kersse a "welsher", someone who doesn't pay. Here the tailor flings it back.
- **Reefer:**
  - A sailor who reefs sails; slang for a midshipman. The *Oxford English Dictionary* cites this very sentence under "reefer".
  - A **reefer jacket**, a sailor's coat, which is the tailor's own product.
  - Danish *røver*, "robber"; reaver or reiver, a raider.
- **wenchman** = Welshman + **wencher**, a skirt-chaser.
- **Taffy elsewhere.** The *Wake* elsewhere attaches the Taffy rhyme to Shem, "the alien" (Hodgart). Later in this same chapter, one of the two comedians who tell the Buckley story is called **Taff**.

### 14. "One can smell off his wetsments how he is coming from a beach of promisck."

**Plain sense:** "You can smell on his clothes that he's come from breaking his promise to some girl on a beach." Campbell & Robinson: "a breach of promise — a beach of promiscuity".

- **wetsments:**
  - Vestments, i.e. garments (French *vêtements*).
  - Wet: sea-soaked, or urine (the "pumps" again).
  - [Speculative] Joyce said this section was "about Gideon", who tested God with a woollen fleece, first wet and then dry (Judges 6). Here the captain's clothes are wet, and the next paragraph opens "Upon this dry call". See §6, point 8.
- **beach of promisck:**
  - **Breach of promise**, the lawsuit for a broken engagement: another kind of *suit*.
  - Beach + **promiscuous**: Sandymount Strand again, the beach of Bloom's misbehaviour in *Ulysses*.
  - Devlin: "a native Irishman's fear of an invader plundering his promising shores".
- **"One can smell"** is the giant's "Fee, fi, fo, fum, I smell the blood of an Englishman", sniffing out a foreigner. "Feof fife" turns up eight lines later.

### 15. "Where is that old muttiny, shall I ask?"

**Plain sense:** "And where's the old lecher got to now, may I ask?"

- **shall I ask?** is the Welsh-English tag from *Taffy*: "What for that tape measure, shall I ask?" In the play, the tape measures a new wife for a dead wife's clothes.
- **muttiny:**
  - **Mutiny**. There is none in Wagner, but some versions of the Dutchman legend have one, including Scott's *Rokeby* (Martin).
  - **Old mutton**, and slang *muttoner*, "a wencher" (McHugh).
  - **Mutt**, of Mutt and Jute (FW 16–18), the book's first native-meets-invader dialogue.
- Campbell & Robinson: "Where is that old mutinous spirit, may I ask?"

### 16. "Free kicks he will have from me, turncoats, in Bar Bartley if I wars a fewd years ago."

**Plain sense:** "He'd get three kicks up the backside from me, the turncoat, if I were a few years younger."

- **Free kicks:**
  - **Three kicks** is the formula of Joyce's father: Stanislaus Joyce's diary records John Joyce's threat to "break his bloody arse with 3 kicks!" (McHugh).
  - *Taffy* has "Three kicks the boy will have".
  - Also a football free kick (Mink indexes it under the Gaelic Athletic Association).
- **turncoats:**
  - A renegade, who will indeed change church a couple of pages later.
  - In tailoring, to "turn" a worn coat is to remake it inside out.
- **Bar Bartley** is not explained in the standard annotations. Candidates:
  - **Barbary**, as in the Barbary corsairs (compare "xebec");
  - the **bar** of this very pub;
  - **Partholón**, the mythical invader of Ireland ("Bartley" anglicises the name);
  - Bartley, the son drowned at sea in Synge's *Riders to the Sea*, which Joyce translated into Italian.
- **if I wars a fewd years ago** = "if I were a few years younger" (McHugh), crossed with "if it were a few years ago" (*Taffy*: "if I was forty years ago"). It adds wars and feud; Norwegian *var* = "was".

### 17. "Meistr Capteen Gaascooker, a salestrimmer!"

**Plain sense:** "*Mister* Captain Goose-Cooker, a sail-trimmer and a time-server!"

- **Meistr:** Welsh *meistr*, "master, Mr", in its exact spelling; German *Meister*, "master", as in master tailor.
- **Capteen:** "captain" in a foreign or stage-Irish mouth. Possibly the Irish diminutive *-een*, making it "little captain".
- **Gaascooker:**
  - Danish/Norwegian ***gaas***, "goose" (Christiani). So "cook his goose" (McHugh), answering "culp me goose" at the start.
  - A gas cooker.
  - The barnacle goose: Nora Barnacle, Joyce's wife (Gordon; Glasheen queries it).
- **salestrimmer:**
  - A sail-trimmer (McHugh). Tindall: "at once sailor and tailor".
  - A tailor's **trimmings**.
  - A political **trimmer**, who trims his sails to every wind: the turncoat again.
  - A salesman.

### 18. "As he was soampling me ledder, like pulp, and as I was trailing his fumbelums, like hulp, he'll fell the fall of me faus, he sazd, like yulp!"

**Plain sense:** "While he was pawing my leather like it was pulp, and I was slaving over his frills like a skivvy… he'll feel the force of my fist, and he'll yelp!"

- **soampling me ledder:**
  - "Sampling my leather" (McHugh): the customer handling the goods.
  - "To leather" someone is to thrash them.
  - **Ladder:** Finnegan's fatal fall from the ladder in the ballad "Finnegan's Wake".
- **trailing his fumbelums:**
  - **Tailoring** his **furbelows** (flounces, frills; McHugh), and fumbling them.
  - **Trailing one's coat** was the classic Donnybrook Fair provocation: you dragged your coat on the ground and dared anyone to tread on it. "Donnerbruch fire" stands six lines above.
- **fell the fall of me faus:**
  - "Feel the force of my fist": German ***Faust*** = "fist".
  - Fell = strike down; also an animal's hide (German *Fell*), after "leather".
  - **The fall**: HCE's Fall.
- **pulp, hulp, yulp** is a three-step ladder of cries:
  - beaten to a **pulp**;
  - ***hulp***, Dutch for "help!" (McHugh's gloss at this point is "like hell!");
  - **yulp**, a yelp, and also Norwegian ***hjelp***, "help", pronounced roughly "yelp".
  - The victim's last cry is in the Norwegian's own language.

### 19. "The goragorridgorballyed pushkalsson, he sazd,"

**Plain sense:** "that gorbellied, mountain-humped son-of-a-gun Humpson".

- **goragorridgorballyed:**
  - **Gorbellied**, pot-bellied (Falstaff's "gorbellied knaves"), plus **gory** and **horrid**.
  - Russian ***gora*** = "mountain" (*gora gor*, "mountain of mountains"); ***gorb*** = "hump" (*gorbatyi*, "hunchbacked").
  - Old English *gor* = "dung".
  - The stammering "gor-a-gor-ridge-gor" builds a mountain range on the man's back.
- **pushkalsson:**
  - **Pukkelsen**, the captain's name in this yarn, from Danish/Norwegian ***pukkel*** = "hump": "Humpson".
  - Russian ***pushka*** = "cannon", so "son of a gun". Maybe also Pushkin.
  - The Russian words look ahead to the chapter's next story, "How Buckley Shot the Russian General", another of John Joyce's yarns.

### 20. "with his bellows pockets fulled of potchtatos and his fox in a stomach,"

**Plain sense:** "with his baggy pockets stuffed with spuds and a wolf in his belly".

- **bellows pockets** is a real tailoring term: patch pockets with pleated, expanding sides (McHugh).
- **fulled** = full + **fulling**, the process that thickens woollen cloth.
- **potchtatos:**
  - Potatoes + patch pockets.
  - Russian ***pochta*** = "post": letters in his pockets? The *Wake* turns around a letter.
  - "Patch-tato", from *Taffy*.
  - Bloom carries a potato as a talisman in *Ulysses*.
- **fox in a stomach:**
  - "A wolf in the stomach", i.e. a glutton (McHugh, citing *Ulysses*).
  - [Speculative] Plutarch's Spartan boy, who let a stolen fox gnaw his belly rather than confess the theft.
- **The tailor's logic:** the man's front bulges too.

### 21. "a disagrees to his ramskew coddlelecherskithers' zirkuvs,"

**Plain sense:** "a disgrace to his Roman Catholic church".

- **In Russian it is *Rimskaya katolicheskaya tserkov'*** (McHugh; Škrabánek), with English stuffed into every syllable:
  - **ramskew** (*Rimsk-*, "Roman") + ram (lust) + **askew** (crooked);
  - **coddlelecherskithers'** (*katolicheska-*) + Dublin **coddle** (a stew) + **lechers** + kith + the skitters;
  - **zirkuvs** (*tserkov'*, "church") + German *Zirkus*, "circus".
- **disagrees** = disgrace, plus the suit that *disagrees* with him, i.e. doesn't fit. [My reading.]
- **The irony:** he isn't a Catholic yet. Two pages later the ship's husband baptises him: "I popetithes thee, Ocean".

### 22. "drop down dead and deaf,"

- **Curse and oath at once.** "May he drop down dead!" is a curse; "may I drop down dead if…" is an oath, which closes the circle opened by "so help me God". *Taffy* has "drop dead and blind".
- **The Ballad.** It echoes the *Wake*'s great mocking song about HCE, "The Ballad of Persse O'Reilly" (FW 44–47). Tindall: "Kersse is Persse O'Reilly". The Ballad already has every charge here:
  - "the brave son of Scandiknavery";
  - "A Norwegian camel old cod";
  - the "hammerfast viking";
  - "With the deaf and dumb Danes, / And all their remains".

### 23. "and there is never a teilwrmans in the feof fife of Iseland or in the wholeabelongd of Skunkinabory"

**Plain sense:** "and there isn't a tailor in all five provinces of Ireland, or in the whole length of Scandinavia". The main clause finally arrives, eighteen lines after the sentence began.

- **teilwrmans:** Welsh ***teilwr*** = "tailor" (the draft has plain "tailorman"), plus German *Teil* = "part".
- **feof fife:**
  - **Five fifths.** The Irish word for a province, *cúige*, means "a fifth", and Ireland once had five.
  - Fife in Scotland; fief or feoff, a feudal holding.
  - **Fee-fi-fo-fum.**
- **Iseland:** Ireland + **Iceland** (Danish *Island*) + Iseult + Welsh/Cornish *isel*, "low".
- **wholeabelongd:**
  - The whole length.
  - "Belong" in its Irish sense: "where do you belong?" means "where are you from?"
  - Hullabaloo (McHugh).
- **Skunkinabory:**
  - **Scandinavia** + **skunk** (the smell again).
  - Norwegian *innabords* = "on board".
  - Compare the Ballad's "Scandiknavery".

### 24. "from Drumadunderry till the rumnants of Mecckrass,"

**Plain sense:** "from Derry to Kerry", one end of Ireland to the other.

- **Drumadunderry:**
  - (London)**derry**, in the north.
  - Irish *Drom an Dúin Doire*, "ridge of the fort of the oak-wood" (O Hehir).
  - Dundrum.
  - Norwegian *dunder* = "thunder", in line with "Donnerbruch".
- **rumnants of Mecckrass:**
  - The **remnants**, i.e. the ruins, of **Muckross Abbey** near Killarney, Co. Kerry, in the south-west. Irish *Muc-ros* = "pig headland".
  - **Ruminants**: camels chewing the cud, with **Mecca**, camel country.
  - A draper's **remnants**.
  - German *meckern* = "to bleat", colloquially "to grouse".

### 25. "could milk a colt in thrushes foran furrow follower width"

**Plain sense:** "could make a coat and trousers for a foreign fellow with" (McHugh).

- **milk a colt / make a coat; thrushes / trousers** (Irish *triús*, "trews"). The sound-swaps keep the shape of the phrase while changing every word. [Speculative] Milking a colt is also an impossible task, like fitting this customer.
- **foran:**
  - Norwegian ***foran*** = "in front" (Christiani); Dublin "forrin" = "foreign".
  - *Foran* (front) is set against *bakk* (back) at the end of the sentence.
- **furrow follower:** fellow + **farrow** + a ploughman, i.e. a landsman, not a sailor.
- **width:** "with" + the tailor's measurement.

### 26. "that a hole in his tale and that hell of a hull of a hill of a camelump bakk."

**Plain sense:** "that hole in his tail and that hell of a hump on his back".

- **hole in his tale:**
  - A hole in his **tail**: the seat of his trousers, his coat-tails, his backside (the hawsehole again).
  - A hole in his **tale**: his story doesn't hold up (Norwegian *tale* = "speech"). The critic Fraser says the story has a hole "because it must go around the hump".
- **hell of a hull of a hill of a:**
  - A vowel ladder: hell → hull → hill.
  - **Hull**: a ship; Norwegian *hul* = "hole".
  - **Hill**: **Howth**, the hill that is HCE's head and hump in the *Wake*'s landscape.
  - The "of a … of a" chain copies the song "Son of a Gambolier" ("I'm a son of a, son of a, son of a…") (Hodgart & Worthington).
- **camelump bakk:**
  - Camel-hump. Danish ***bakke*** = "hill"; Norwegian ***bak*** = "behind"; the draft's plain "back".
  - The camel has been coming since "ship of the desert". The tailor's joke is the Gospel's: "It is easier for a camel to go through the eye of a needle…" (Matthew 19:24). Compare "bump where the camel got the needle" (FW 494).
  - ALP calls HCE "my much-altered camel's hump" (FW 201), and "altered" is a tailor's word.
- **The drafts trace this line:** "that camelump back" (1936) → "bakk" (Dec 1936) → the hole, hell, hull and hill added on the 1938 galleys.

### 27. "Fadgestfudgist!"

**Plain sense:** "Fit him? Fudge!"

- **The two words:**
  - **fadge** = "to fit, suit"; Viola in *Twelfth Night* asks "How will this fadge?";
  - **fudge** = "to botch, fake, patch up", and "Fudge!" = "nonsense".
- **The whole quarrel in one word:** *fit* against *botch*, the captain's charge against the tailor's, in one exploding superlative ("the fittest-fudgiest!").
- **Other layers:**
  - "Fascist!" (McHugh; it was 1937);
  - Latin *fiat* ("let it be made") and *fuit* ("it was");
  - Thomas Moore's satire *The Fudge Family in Paris*;
  - Captain Fudge, the lying sea captain whose name is said to have given "fudge" its meaning;
  - [Speculative] Ulster *fadge*, potato bread, which would pick up the potatoes.
- **The third cry.** With "salestrimmer!" and "yulp!" it makes the paragraph's third exclamation mark, and Joyce added two of the three after 1937. FinnegansWiki reads the next sentence's "dry call" as German *drei*, "three": three calls, like Roland's horn ("Riland's in peril!").
- **What comes next.** Added in December 1937, it is the paragraph's last word. The next paragraph opens with a thunderclap: "Upon this dry call of selenium cell … with its doomed crack of the old damn ukonnen power…".
  - The tailor's curse turns into the radio's thunder-static. In the *Wake*, thunder is Vico's thunder, the noise of the Fall (Finnish *ukkonen* = "thunder").
  - HCE, the publican, comes back to his customers and feels "their joke was coming home to them".

## 6. What the paragraph is doing

**1. Each man insults the other in the other's trade.** This is the second half of a *flyting*, a formal exchange of abuse. The captain cursed the tailor in tailor's words: gusset, worsted, needle, cloth. The tailor curses back in sailor's words: buccaneer, corsair, xebec, reefer, mutineer. The pub has already named the game: "tig for tager … strop for stripe", tit for tat. Tindall calls sailor and tailor "twins of a sort". Joyce fuses the two vocabularies so tightly that many words belong to both: pumps, reefer, togs/tugs, trimmer, hull/hole. A row about a suit becomes land against sea.

**2. A quarrel about fault.** The paragraph opens with *culpa* ("culp me goose") and closes with *fadge*, "fit". Between the two sits the question in John Joyce's anecdote: when a suit doesn't fit, whose fault is it, the needle's or the body's? The *Wake* asks the same of HCE. Is his fall his own sin, or other people's gossip? The book's answer is *O felix culpa*, the happy fault, because the fall starts the cycle over again ("recoursing").

**3. Native against invader, with the joke on the native.** Ellmann called the tale one of "the parables of native and outlander". The insults are a crash course in Viking Ireland: Eric Bloodaxe, Harald Bluetooth, Hardrada's land-ravager banner, the Danish flag, a foreigner creeping into "our raw lenguage". The punchline covers the island from Derry to Kerry. Anthony Burgess: "The villain is evidently HCE, foreigner, invader, cheat, lecher." But the native is compromised three times over:
- He curses the foreigner in Russian, Norwegian, Danish, Dutch, German and Welsh: "a towering polyglottal rage" (Leeming). The language he defends is already made of the invader's.
- He has just come back in a sailor's rig himself.
- The yarn ends with the foreigner baptised, married to a local girl, and "converted to a landshop". Dublin was a Viking town, and the invader becomes the founding father.

**4. A son cursing his father.** The tailor plays Ham, the son who exposes his drunken father's nakedness. A tailor is, after all, the man who sees his customers undressed.
- The young men in the pub have already mimicked the hump ("Hump! Hump!", 312).
- They are telling a story about a hunchbacked Scandinavian to a hunchbacked, Scandinavian-descended publican. Right after this paragraph he comes back to the bar and feels "their joke was coming home to them".
- The tirade reprises the Ballad of Persse O'Reilly, the street song that ruins HCE in Book I. Kersse is Persse.

**5. Clothes are identity.** A suit is a garment, a courtship ("voyaging after maidens") and a lawsuit ("breach of promise"). A turncoat changes church. A man who won't fit his suit won't fit in. John Bishop: the episode "engages the dark questions 'Who fits?' and 'Who is suitable?'". Behind it stands Carlyle's *Sartor Resartus* ("the tailor re-tailored"), which argues that society is held together by clothes. The yarn quotes it as "sartor's risorted" (314.17).

**6. The body as land and ship.** The hump is a mountain (*gora*), a ridge (*drom*), a hill (*bakke*, and Howth, HCE's head in the *Wake*'s landscape), and a hull. The captain is at once a leaking ship (pumps, hull, hawsehole) and a badly cut suit (collars, cuffs, pockets, tail).

**7. The Fall, with thunder.** "He'll fell the fall", the ladder, *Donnerbruch* (thunder-break), *Drumadunderry* (Norwegian *dunder*, thunder). Then the next paragraph's "doomed crack of the old damn ukonnen power", from Finnish *ukkonen*, thunder. In the *Wake*, thunder is God's voice in Vico's cycle, the sound of every fall. The tailor's curse becomes the thunderclap that restarts the round.

**8. Gideon.** *[My own reading, building on Fritz Senn's work; I haven't found it in print.]* In December 1936, while adding most of this paragraph, Joyce told a friend that the section was "chiefly or in great part about — Gideon. (Book of Judges, chap. 6, verse 36 et seq.)". Senn showed that Kersse, "Son of Joe Ashe", is Gideon son of Joash, and that "And it was so. Behold." (322.24), ten lines before this paragraph, quotes the fleece story. Read against the Book of Judges, three things line up:
- Gideon drove out desert raiders whose "camels were without number, as the sand by the sea side for multitude" (Judges 7:12). The tailor curses his foreign enemy with desert sand and a camel's hump.
- Gideon tested God with a woollen fleece, first wet, then dry. The captain's clothes are "wetsments", and the next paragraph begins "Upon this dry call".
- Gideon's three companies routed the enemy camp by blowing horns, smashing pitchers and uncovering lamps (Judges 7:16–20). The tirade ends on its third exclamation mark, and the next sentence has a "horn", a "doomed crack" and a "selenium cell" (a cell that reacts to light).

**9. The father's story, told by the son.** The voice is John Joyce's: his "three kicks", his bluster, his oaths. The yarn passed from Joyce's godfather to his father to him, and the son turned it into a curse on a father no tailor can fit. Joyce knew his father's verdict in advance: "Well, he can't tell that story as I used to." *Fadgestfudgist* (fits, botched, let it be, it was) is the last word on the quarrel, and perhaps on Joyce's own patchwork book.

## 7. How sure is this?

- **Solid** (in the standard annotations or the drafts):
  - the plot frame and the "said" code;
  - Chapelizod, the Flying Dutchman and Beau Brummell;
  - the Russian words (*portnoi*, *pushka*, the church);
  - Pukkelsen from *pukkel*; the Taffy rhyme;
  - "make a coat and trousers for a foreign fellow"; Derry to Muckross; the five fifths;
  - fadge and fudge;
  - the draft history; Worsaae and *Taffy* as sources.
- **Likely** (strong, but not found in print):
  - Ham alongside O'Connell;
  - "re-cursing" = Kersse;
  - "disagrees" = doesn't fit;
  - Babel in "confound";
  - the camel-and-needle joke;
  - Norwegian *hjelp* in "yulp";
  - the Danebrog legend behind "Donnerbruch".
- **Speculative** (flagged above):
  - the Gideon parallels in §6;
  - the Paul Jones readings;
  - the Spartan boy's fox;
  - Ulster fadge.
- **Unsolved:** "Bar Bartley" and "polly joans" (no standard gloss for either).
