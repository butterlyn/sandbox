## 1. Can "what" be separated from "how"? (requirements vs. design)

### Takeaway
Almost nobody now defends finishing requirements before design and independently of it. The mainstream view (intertwining, Twin Peaks, problem–solution co-evolution) is that problem understanding and solution exploration develop together. What survives, and is still taught, is a separation of *descriptions*: state the required effects in the problem world, keep design decisions out unless they are deliberate constraints, and accept that the what/how line is relative. Two challenges are still live. The architecture community argues that architecturally significant requirements cannot be told apart from architectural decisions. A newer experimental strand (Ralph; Mohanani et al.) finds that merely *calling* desiderata "requirements" makes designers fixate on them and reduces originality.

### Cited Findings

#### Position A: requirements state "what", not "how" (separate the descriptions)
- **Ross, D.T. & Schoman, K.E. (1977). "Structured Analysis for Requirements Definition." *IEEE Transactions on Software Engineering* SE-3(1):6–15.** This is one of the founding statements of requirements definition as a distinct activity. As indexed, the abstract proposes three interrelated subjects: context analysis, functional specification and design constraints. I verified only this abstract-level claim, via search; I did not open the full text. — [doi:10.1109/TSE.1977.229899](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1977.229899)
- ★ **Zave, P. & Jackson, M. (1997). "Four dark corners of requirements engineering." *ACM TOSEM* 6(1):1–30.** The canonical modern basis for separating requirement from design. It argues that "all descriptions involved in requirements engineering should be descriptions of the environment", explains "the close association between domain knowledge and refinement of requirements", and sets out "the precise nature of requirements, specifications, and domain knowledge, as well as the precise nature of the relationships among them", establishing "minimum standards for what information should be represented in a requirements language" (abstract). — [doi:10.1145/237432.237434](https://doi.org/10.1145/237432.237434)
- **Meyer, B. (1985). "On formalism in specifications." *IEEE Software* 2(1):6–26.** Its "seven sins of the specifier" are noise, silence, over-specification, contradiction, ambiguity, forward reference and wishful thinking. "Over-specification" is exactly the what/how failure: "The reader is told too much about the solution while he or she is desperately trying to grasp the problem." — [doi:10.1109/MS.1985.229776](https://doi.org/10.1109/MS.1985.229776); support (summary that quotes the paper): [The Morning Paper, 2016](https://blog.acolyer.org/2016/11/08/on-formalisms-in-specifications/)
- ★ **Parnas, D.L. & Clements, P.C. (1986). "A rational design process: How and why to fake it." *IEEE TSE* SE-12(2):251–257.** Bridges the two camps. In the ideal process a requirements document comes first: "Completion of this document before we start would allow us to design with all the requirements in front of us". But "We will never find a process that allows us to design software in a perfectly rational way. The good news is that we can fake it." Their reasons include: clients "do not know exactly what they want and are unable to tell us all that they know"; "Many of the details only become known to us as we progress in the implementation"; external change; "preconceived design ideas"; and reuse of software built for other projects. — [doi:10.1109/TSE.1986.6312940](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.1986.6312940); support (full text): [course-hosted copy, Texas State](https://userweb.cs.txstate.edu/~rp31/papers/Fake_it.pdf)

#### Position B: specification and implementation are inevitably intertwined
- ★ **Swartout, W. & Balzer, R. (1982). "On the inevitable intertwining of specification and implementation." *Communications of the ACM* 25(7):438–440.** "Contrary to recent claims that specification should be completed before implementation begins," the paper argues "the two processes must be intertwined", for two reasons. First, limits of the implementation technology can force a specification change: implementing a stack as an array imposes a depth limit. Second, implementation choices suggest additions to the specification: reusing a pattern-matcher adds wildcard search (abstract). — [doi:10.1145/358557.358572](https://doi.org/10.1145/358557.358572)
- **Brooks, F.P. (1987). "No Silver Bullet: Essence and Accidents of Software Engineering." *Computer* 20(4):10–19.** "The hardest single part of building a software system is deciding precisely what to build… the most important function that software builders do for their clients is the iterative extraction and refinement of the product requirements. For the truth is, the clients do not know what they want." — [doi:10.1109/MC.1987.1663532](https://doi.org/10.1109/MC.1987.1663532); support (full text): [hosted copy](http://worrydream.com/refs/Brooks-NoSilverBullet.pdf)
- **Curtis, B., Krasner, H. & Iscoe, N. (1988). "A field study of the software design process for large systems." *CACM* 31(11):1268–1287.** Based on interviews from 17 large projects. "Fluctuating and conflicting requirements" was one of three problems (with the thin spread of application-domain knowledge and communication breakdowns) that hurt productivity and quality (abstract). — [doi:10.1145/50087.50089](https://doi.org/10.1145/50087.50089)
- **Rittel, H.W.J. & Webber, M.M. (1973). "Dilemmas in a general theory of planning." *Policy Sciences* 4(2):155–169.** The origin of "wicked problems", which they contrast with "tame" ones. The listed characteristics include: "There is no definitive formulation of a wicked problem"; "Wicked problems have no stopping rule"; solutions are "not true-or-false, but good or bad"; and "The choice of explanation determines the nature of the problem's resolution." This is the conceptual root of the claim that you cannot fully state the problem without engaging with solutions. — [doi:10.1007/BF01405730](https://doi.org/10.1007/BF01405730); support (secondary summary listing the properties): [Università Ca' Foscari handout](https://www.unive.it/pag/fileadmin/user_upload/inglese/tutorato/Wicked_Problems.pdf)
- ★ **Dorst, K. & Cross, N. (2001). "Creativity in the design process: co-evolution of problem–solution." *Design Studies* 22(5):425–437.** Empirical design-research evidence: protocol studies of nine experienced industrial designers, whose designs were rated for quality and creativity. The study tests a model of creative design as "co-evolution of problem/solution spaces", finds it broadly valid, and adds "default" and "surprise" problem/solution spaces. — [doi:10.1016/S0142-694X(01)00009-6](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0142-694X%2801%2900009-6); support (abstract summary): [TU/e research portal](https://research.tue.nl/en/publications/creativity-in-the-design-process-co-evolution-of-problem-solution/)
- ★ **Nuseibeh, B. (2001). "Weaving together requirements and architectures." *Computer* 34(3):115–119.** The "Twin Peaks" model. Its companion workshop paper (STRAW'01) states: "The cornerstone of such a process is that a system's requirements and its architecture are developed concurrently." They are interleaved in a finer-grained spiral, but their "distinct content is preserved." It argues waterfall-style ordering freezes requirements too early or lets architecture constrain users, and it addresses Boehm's "I'll Know It When I See It", COTS selection, and rapid change through stable "core" requirements. — [doi:10.1109/2.910904](https://doi.org/10.1109/2.910904); support (companion paper I opened: Nuseibeh, "Weaving the Software Development Process Between Requirements and Architectures", STRAW'01): [STRAW'01 e-paper](https://www.cin.ufpe.br/~straw01/epapers/paper05/paper05.html)
- **Cleland-Huang, J., Hanmer, R.S., Supakkul, S. & Mirakhorli, M. (2013). "The Twin Peaks of Requirements and Architecture." *IEEE Software* 30(2):24–29.** The guest editors' introduction to an *IEEE Software* special issue on the requirements–architecture interplay. Crossref also records a dedicated TwinPeaks workshop series, from the "First IEEE International Workshop on the Twin Peaks of Requirements and Architecture" (2012) to its 5th edition (2015). — [doi:10.1109/MS.2013.39](https://doi.org/10.1109/MS.2013.39)

#### Position C (architecture side): architecturally significant requirements *are* design decisions
- ★ **de Boer, R.C. & van Vliet, H. (2009). "On the similarity between requirements and architecture." *Journal of Systems and Software* 82(3):544–550.** Once architecture is viewed as a set of design decisions rather than a structure, "there is no fundamental distinction between architectural decisions and architecturally significant requirements"; the authors call for closer cooperation between the two communities (abstract, accepted manuscript). — [doi:10.1016/j.jss.2008.11.185](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jss.2008.11.185); support: [accepted manuscript, VU Amsterdam](http://www.cs.vu.nl/~hans/publications/y2004/SA-RE.pdf)
- ★ **Chen, L., Ali Babar, M. & Nuseibeh, B. (2013). "Characterizing Architecturally Significant Requirements." *IEEE Software* 30(2):38–45.** A grounded-theory study based on interviews with 90 practitioners (1,448 years of combined experience, more than 500 organisations). It offers a framework for characterising ASRs, "allowing the twin peaks to move from aspiration to reality" (abstract). — [doi:10.1109/MS.2012.174](https://doi.org/10.1109/MS.2012.174)

#### Position D: "requirements" is an illusion, and the framing itself causes harm
- ★ **Ralph, P. (2013). "The illusion of requirements in software development." *Requirements Engineering* 18(3):293–296.** Viewpoint. When a problem has radically different candidate solutions, analysts can state a goal, but "more specific desiderata are contingent on the chosen solution"; in such "situations of sparse requirements, analysts may misrepresent design decisions as requirements, creating an illusion of requirements" (abstract). — [doi:10.1007/s00766-012-0161-4](https://doi.org/10.1007/s00766-012-0161-4)
- **Ralph, P. (2016). "Software engineering process theory: A multi-method comparison of Sensemaking–Coevolution–Implementation Theory and Function–Behavior–Structure Theory." *Information and Software Technology* 70:232–250.** Combines a survey of more than 1,300 developers with four longitudinal case studies. It supports Sensemaking–Coevolution–Implementation theory over the Function–Behavior–Structure framework: development is better described as "ad hoc oscillation" between sensemaking, co-evolution of the context and the design space, and implementation, rather than iteration "between weakly-coupled phases (analysis, design, coding, testing)" (abstract). — [doi:10.1016/j.infsof.2015.06.010](https://doi.org/10.1016/j.infsof.2015.06.010)
- ★ **Mohanani, R., Ralph, P. & Shreeve, B. (2014). "Requirements fixation." *ICSE 2014*, 895–906.** The original experiment: "labelling desiderata as requirements led to less original designs", as summarised by the authors in the 2021 follow-up. — [doi:10.1145/2568225.2568235](https://doi.org/10.1145/2568225.2568235); support: [arXiv version of the 2021 TSE paper](https://arxiv.org/abs/1902.11278)
- ★ **Mohanani, R., Turhan, B. & Ralph, P. (2021). "Requirements Framing Affects Design Creativity." *IEEE TSE* 47(5):936–947.** Re-analyses the 2014 data and adds a second experiment, giving two randomised controlled experiments with 42 and 34 participants. Participants given desiderata framed as "requirements" or "prioritized requirements" produced designs that were "on average, less original but more practical" than those given the same desiderata framed as "ideas"; "design performance is highly susceptible to minor changes in the vernacular used to communicate desiderata" (abstract). — [doi:10.1109/TSE.2019.2909033](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2019.2909033)
- **Mohanani, R., Ralph, P., Turhan, B. & Mandić, V. (2022). "How Templated Requirements Specifications Inhibit Creativity in Software Engineering." *IEEE TSE* 48(10):4074–4086.** A protocol study with 42 designers working in 21 pairs. Designers "tend to accept given requirements and priority levels while rejecting newer, more innovative design ideas"; "requirements fixation mediates the negative relationship between specification formality and creativity" (abstract). — [doi:10.1109/TSE.2021.3112503](https://doi.org/10.1109/TSE.2021.3112503)
- **Ralph, P. & Mohanani, R. (2015). "Is Requirements Engineering Inherently Counterproductive?" *5th Int. Workshop on the Twin Peaks of Requirements and Architecture*, 20–23.** Listed only for its title and venue, which show the position being argued directly at the Twin Peaks workshop; I could not retrieve its abstract. — [doi:10.1109/TwinPeaks.2015.12](https://doi.org/10.1109/TwinPeaks.2015.12)

#### Where the debate stands (2026)
- Practitioner certification now builds intertwining in. Secondary descriptions of the IREB CPRE Foundation Level Syllabus 3.0 (available since 1 October 2020) list among its nine principles "Problem – Requirement – Solution", whose three aspects "must be intertwined". This comes from a search-engine extract of a secondary page; I did not open the primary IREB syllabus. — [t2informatik "What is CPRE?"](https://t2informatik.de/en/smartpedia/cpre/)
- The Twin Peaks programme has empirical grounding (Chen et al. 2013, above). The requirements-fixation programme had published three peer-reviewed studies by 2022 (above). I found no published refutation of the fixation findings, though I did not search exhaustively.

### Inferences
- The live disagreement is no longer "separate vs. intertwine" in *process*. It is about (a) whether "requirement" and "design decision" differ in *kind* (Zave & Jackson, and Twin Peaks' "distinct content is preserved", say yes; de Boer & van Vliet for ASRs, and Ralph, say no or not always), and (b) how firmly desiderata should be presented to designers (Mohanani et al.).
- The what/how distinction is best read as a *decision-rights boundary* between the parties, not a property of a sentence. "Over-specification" (Meyer) means fixing something the receiving party should decide. Where that boundary lies is a contractual and organisational choice. This is my synthesis of Meyer 1985, Nuseibeh 2001 and de Boer & van Vliet 2009, and it fits the secondary attribution of the "what-versus-how paradox" to Davis (see Gaps).
- Implications for someone who specifies requirements and leaves design to others (my synthesis, not a finding of any single study):
  1. Write requirements as observable effects in the environment or at the system boundary, and record domain assumptions explicitly (Zave & Jackson).
  2. Separate genuine constraints, with their rationale, from solution ideas. Mark ideas as ideas, because the framing changes what designers produce (Mohanani et al. 2021/2022).
  3. Expect to iterate with architects on quality attributes and ASRs, where the boundary is thinnest (Nuseibeh; Chen et al.; de Boer & van Vliet).
  4. Write and maintain the requirements document *as if* the process were rational, even though it is not (Parnas & Clements).
- The fixation evidence comes from experiments and protocol studies, not field outcomes. It shows an effect on originality but also more *practical* designs under "requirements" framing (Mohanani et al. 2021), so it is a trade-off, not a verdict against specification.

## 2. How much should be specified up front?

### Takeaway
The debate has moved from "big upfront requirements vs. working software" to a context- and risk-driven middle. Boehm & Turner's risk-based balancing and Glinz & Fricker's "rely on implicit shared understanding only when the risk is low" are the canonical frames. Field evidence shows agile teams still produce and rely on requirements artefacts, mostly free text, plus tests used as requirements. The pain points are the same as in plan-driven work: incomplete or hidden requirements and communication flaws. User stories remain the sharpest disagreement. Patton treats them as prompts for conversation; Meyer argues they lack the abstraction requirements need.

### Inferences
- Both camps now agree, in substance, that the amount of upfront specification should scale with risk: criticality, size, distance between parties, and turnover (Boehm & Turner; Glinz & Fricker). The remaining disagreement is about defaults and about whether user stories are an adequate *requirements* notation (Meyer) or a deliberately incomplete *conversation* token (Patton).
- "Agile = no requirements documentation" is not what surveys find. Agile organisations document requirements, mostly as free text, and often as tests (Wagner et al. 2017; Wagenaar et al. 2018; Bjarnason et al. 2016).
- For a specialist who hands requirements to separate designers and implementers, the Glinz & Fricker logic points toward more explicit documentation. Implicit shared understanding is weakest exactly when the specifier and the builders are different people (my inference).

### Gaps
- I could not open Zhi et al. (2015) for its quantitative findings on documentation cost and benefit, so no numbers are reported.

## 3. Natural language vs. formal or model-based specification

### Takeaway
Natural language still dominates practice, often with templates, and its typical defects (ambiguity, incompleteness, inconsistency) are the most-reported specification problems. Advocates have argued since the 1980s that formal methods are unfairly mythologised. The best survey evidence (Woodcock et al. 2009; Gleirscher & Marmsoler 2020) reports perceived quality gains and usefulness, but also a negative perceived ease of use and a skills barrier. High-profile industrial cases (AWS and TLA+) show value for precise design specification. Controlled natural language such as EARS is the pragmatic middle ground. One caution from the fixation research: more formal or templated presentation of desiderata can reduce design creativity.

### Inferences
- The evidence for formal methods is real, but it comes from self-selected adopters. Woodcock et al. surveyed projects already "known… to have employed formal techniques", and AWS is a single, unusually capable organisation. It shows that formal methods *can* pay off; it does not show they pay off typically.
- The adoption barrier is now framed as usability and skills, not usefulness (Gleirscher & Marmsoler), which is why controlled natural language like EARS and templates are where practice actually is (Franch et al. 2023).
- For a requirements specialist there is a real tension between precision (formal or templated statements, which reduce the ambiguity practitioners report most) and openness (the fixation findings). A plausible resolution, which is my inference, is to be precise about *problem-world* properties and acceptance conditions while explicitly marking which statements are negotiable ideas.

- I found no independent, non-self-selected comparison of NL vs. formal requirements on project outcomes.
- The EARS adoption list is self-reported on the author's site.

## 4. Is "non-functional requirement" a coherent category, and what makes a requirement "good"? (brief; another researcher covers quality)

### Takeaway
The functional/non-functional split is contested. Glinz (2007) documented that there is no consensus definition. Eckhardt et al. (2016) found that most industrial "NFRs" actually describe behaviour and can be handled like functional requirements. On quality, the newer view (Femmer & Vogelsang 2019) defines a "good" requirement by how well it serves its downstream users (quality in use), not by a fixed checklist.

### Cited Findings
- **Glinz, M. (2007). "On Non-Functional Requirements." *RE'07*, 21–26.** "Although the term 'non-functional requirement' has been in use for more than 20 years, there is still no consensus in the requirements engineering community what non-functional requirements are and how we should elicit, document, and validate them", yet there is "a unanimous consensus that non-functional requirements are important" (abstract). The paper surveys definitions, discusses their problems, and proposes concepts to overcome them. — [doi:10.1109/RE.2007.45](https://doi.org/10.1109/RE.2007.45); support: [author's PDF (UZH)](https://files.ifi.uzh.ch/rerg/amadeus/publications/papers/RE07_Glinz.pdf)
- ★ **Eckhardt, J., Vogelsang, A. & Méndez Fernández, D. (2016). "Are 'non-functional' requirements really non-functional? An investigation of non-functional requirements in practice." *ICSE 2016*, 832–842.** Classified 530 NFRs from 11 industrial specifications. "Most 'non-functional' requirements are not non-functional as they describe behavior of a system", so "many so-called NFRs can be handled similarly to functional requirements" (abstract). — [doi:10.1145/2884781.2884788](https://doi.org/10.1145/2884781.2884788)
- **Femmer, H. & Vogelsang, A. (2019). "Requirements Quality Is Quality in Use." *IEEE Software* 36(3):83–91.** "Creating a requirements engineering artifact is rarely an end in itself… such an artifact's purpose is to support the stakeholders in whatever activities they're performing in the project" (abstract). — [doi:10.1109/MS.2018.110161823](https://doi.org/10.1109/MS.2018.110161823)

### Inferences
- The NFR debate connects to the what/how debate. Many quality requirements are where architecture gets decided (ASRs, Q1), and Eckhardt et al. suggest that writing them as observable behaviour makes them more testable and less "vague". That is consistent with stating requirements as environment-level effects (my inference).

### Gaps
- This was deliberately brief; I did not survey the requirements-quality literature (e.g., smells, standards' quality attributes).

## 5. How solid is the evidence base for RE's importance?

### Takeaway
The two most-quoted pillars are weak. The Standish CHAOS figures were heavily criticised for undisclosed methodology, biased sampling and misleading definitions. The "100× cost-to-fix" curve holds, if at all, mainly for severe defects in some contexts, and the largest test of it (171 projects) found no general delayed-issue effect. Better-designed evidence still supports RE's importance more modestly. Incomplete or hidden requirements and customer-communication flaws are the most frequently cited RE problems and are often named as causes of project failure. "Too many requirements and scope changes" was the top reason for cancellation in one replicated survey.

## 6. The research–practice gap

### Takeaway
Leading RE researchers raised the gap themselves around 2002 (Davis & Hickey; Kaindl et al.). The best direct measurement (Franch et al. 2022: 153 practitioners rating 435 papers) is more positive than the folklore. Most ratings were "essential" or "worthwhile", though non-positive ratings are higher than the authors would like, and practitioners keep asking for work on basics such as elicitation and requirement-quality criteria. The flagship research products show limited uptake: goal models (acknowledged in Horkoff et al.'s 2026 reflection) and formal methods (Q3).

## 7. Who should own requirements: dedicated analysts, product owners, or the whole team?

### Takeaway
Scrum assigns accountability to a single Product Owner, who "may delegate" the work. The agile manifesto puts business people and developers in daily collaboration. Empirical studies show the customer/PO role is demanding and, at scale, is in practice a *team* (PO teams, customer teams), while large-scale agile organisations report unclear RE responsibilities. The customer-proxy problem has no settled answer. NaPiRE's data, where communication flaws between team and customer are the most often failure-linked problem, suggests the interface between the people who own requirements and those who build matters more than the job title.

"A Mapping Study on Product Owners in Industry: Identifying Future Research Directions." *ICSSP 2019*, 135–144.** Thirty contributions across seven topics. The PO role "is perceived as difficult in industry"; "the PO role in large-scale projects [is] clearly defined as a group effort, but questions regarding the leadership and management responsibilities of POs remain unanswered" (abstract). — [doi:10.1109/ICSSP.2019.00026](https://doi.org/10.1109/ICSSP.2019.00026)

"How product owner teams scale agile methods to large distributed enterprises." *Empirical Software Engineering* 20(6):1525–1557.** Describes PO *teams* in large offshore programmes. Per Semantic Scholar's machine-generated summary, which I did not check against the abstract, it identifies nine PO team functions. — [doi:10.1007/s10664-014-9322-z](https://doi.org/10.1007/s10664-014-9322-z)

- **Sedano, Ralph & Péraire (2019)** (Q2): the Pivotal study interviewed software engineers, product designers and product managers. The backlog is a boundary object "that helps bridge the gap between the processes of generating user stories and realizing them in working code" (abstract). ### Inferences
- There are three ownership models: a dedicated analyst who writes a specification, a single accountable PO (who may delegate), and whole-team co-creation. The field has not converged; evidence favours *accountable individual + supporting team* at scale (Unger-Windeler et al.; Bass).
- For a reader who wants to specify requirements without designing, the most compatible agile arrangement is working inside a PO team, or as the person to whom the PO delegates backlog content, while keeping explicit artefacts where the risk of misunderstanding is high (Glinz & Fricker, Q2). This is my inference.
