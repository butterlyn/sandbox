# Review Notes: Diet Principles

These notes explain each change to [diet-principles.md](diet-principles.md). Twelve research subagents produced them in October 2026:
- **Six reviewers** each covered a group of the original points.
- **Four researchers** looked for gaps:
  - official guidelines (Australia, NZ, UK, WHO, US 2020–25 and 2025–30, Canada, Brazil, the Nordic countries)
  - large evidence syntheses
  - research on eating habits
  - a sceptic checking for contested or overstated claims
- **Two more** later researched ways to sweeten without added sugar.

**The standard:** a point belongs on the list if official dietary guidelines widely endorse it and systematic evidence supports it. Popular ideas with weak or contested evidence are left out (see [Considered but not added](#considered-but-not-added)).

## Existing points

### Prefer whole foods (reworded)
- **Why it stays:** the link between ultra-processed food and poor health is consistent.
  - An umbrella review covering about 9.9 million people found associations with 32 outcomes (Lane 2024).
  - Two controlled feeding trials found people eat more, or lose less weight, on ultra-processed diets. In Hall 2019 they ate about 500 kcal a day more; the UPDATE trial (2025) is the second.
- **Why it was reworded:** "processed" alone is the wrong line to draw.
  - Most of the evidence is low quality.
  - Harm clusters in processed meat and sugary drinks.
  - Wholegrain bread, breakfast cereals and yoghurt were neutral or linked with lower risk in large cohorts.
  - The UK's Scientific Advisory Committee on Nutrition (SACN, 2025) says it is still unclear whether processing itself is the problem.
  - The new wording says plainly that convenient staples count, and names the foods to rely on less.
- **Sources:** [Lane et al., BMJ 2024](https://www.bmj.com/content/384/bmj-2023-077310) · [Fang et al., BMJ 2024](https://doi.org/10.1136/bmj-2023-078476) · [SACN rapid evidence update, 2025](https://www.gov.uk/government/publications/processed-foods-and-health-sacns-rapid-evidence-update/processed-foods-and-health-sacns-rapid-evidence-update-summary)

### Prioritise fibre → Choose wholegrains
- **The evidence:**
  - High fibre intake is linked with 15–30% lower rates of death, heart disease, stroke, type 2 diabetes and bowel cancer.
  - The benefit is clearest at 25–29 g a day or more, and the authors recommend swapping refined grains for wholegrains (Reynolds 2019, commissioned by the WHO).
- **The gap:**
  - Low wholegrain intake is one of the top two dietary risks worldwide (Global Burden of Disease 2017).
  - In Australia only 23.9% of grain foods eaten are wholegrain, against at least two-thirds recommended (ABS 2023).
- **Why re-scoped:**
  - The old title named a nutrient while the text named foods.
  - Legumes aren't a substitute for a refined food.
  - The list had no point about grains at all.
  - Vegetables, fruit, legumes and nuts already supply fibre through their own points.
- **Sources:** [Reynolds et al., Lancet 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(18)31809-9) · [GBD 2017 Diet Collaborators, Lancet 2019](https://doi.org/10.1016/S0140-6736(19)30041-8) · [ABS, food groups 2023](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/food-and-nutrition/consumption-food-groups-australian-dietary-guidelines/2023)

### Eat more vegetables (reworded)
- **Quantity is the main gap.**
  - Only 4.8% of Australian adults meet the recommended 5–6 serves a day, and the average is about 3 (ABS 2023).
  - Risk keeps falling as intake rises, up to about 800 g a day of fruit and vegetables combined (Aune 2017).
- **"Half your plate"** gives a cue at every meal (Canada, Harvard). "Aim for greater variety" moved to the variety point.
- **The benefit comes mainly from non-starchy vegetables,** such as leafy greens and the cabbage family. Potatoes show no mortality benefit, and chips are linked to higher diabetes risk.
- **Sources:** [Aune et al., Int J Epidemiol 2017](https://academic.oup.com/ije/article/46/3/1029/3039477) · [Wang et al., Circulation 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8084888/) · [ABS, food groups 2023](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/food-and-nutrition/consumption-food-groups-australian-dietary-guidelines/latest-release)

### Eat more fruit (reworded)
- **Two serves a day** matches the Australian guidelines. It is also the intake linked with the lowest mortality in large cohorts: about 2 fruit plus 3 vegetables (Wang 2021). Only 15.9% of Australian adults meet it (ABS 2023).
- **Whole fruit is what counts.**
  - The WHO classes juice as free sugar, and juice shows no mortality benefit, so juice is now covered by the drinks point.
  - Worry about the sugar in whole fruit is unfounded.
- **Sources:** [WHO, Healthy diet fact sheet (Jan 2026)](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet) · [Muraki et al., BMJ 2013](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3978819/)

### Choose healthier fats → Swap to healthier fats
- **The benefit comes from *replacing* saturated fat, not from adding fat.**
  - Cutting saturated fat for at least two years reduced cardiovascular events by about 17% (Cochrane, Hooper 2020).
  - The WHO (2023) caps saturated fat at 10% of energy. Australians average 12.5% (ABS 2023), so the useful message is "swap", not "add".
- **Canola and other plant oils are named alongside olive oil.**
  - The trials used vegetable oils.
  - Higher blood levels of linoleic acid, the main fat in seed oils, go with less heart disease.
  - Claims that seed oils are harmful aren't supported.
- **Nuts, avocado and fish** are covered by other points.
- **Dairy fat is left neutral** because the evidence is genuinely unsettled.
- **Sources:** [Cochrane, Hooper 2020](https://www.cochrane.org/evidence/CD011737) · [WHO fats and carbohydrates guidelines, 2023](https://www.who.int/news/item/17-07-2023-who-updates-guidelines-on-fats-and-carbohydrates) · [Marklund et al., Circulation 2019](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6582360/)

### Improve protein quality → Choose healthier proteins
- **The old title could mislead.** "Protein quality" is a technical measure of amino-acid content and digestibility, on which animal proteins score highest. So the title could read as "eat more animal protein", the opposite of what was meant.
- **Processed meat is the biggest lever.** The WHO's cancer agency (IARC) classes it as a known cause of cancer: each 50 g a day raises bowel-cancer risk by about 18%.
- **Red meat is moderated, not cut.**
  - IARC classes red meat as probably carcinogenic. If the link is causal, each 100 g a day raises bowel-cancer risk by about 17%.
  - Links with type 2 diabetes, heart disease and early death are modest, about 10% higher risk per 100 g a day. That evidence is weaker and contested: the Burden of Proof analysis (2022) rated it weak.
  - Guidelines set a cap, not a ban. The Australian guidelines allow up to 455 g cooked a week, the Heart Foundation under 350 g, and the Nordic guidelines about 350 g. That's a few meals a week.
  - Red meat is a valuable source of well-absorbed iron, zinc and B12. That matters most for people at risk of iron deficiency.
- **Rotating protein sources captures the benefit of the swap.**
  - Replacing a small share of calories from red meat with plant protein was linked with about 12% lower mortality (Song 2016).
  - Plant protein overall is linked with lower mortality; animal protein is neutral (Naghshi 2020).
  - Fish about twice a week is standard advice (NHS, Heart Foundation, Nordic Nutrition Recommendations 2023).
  - Eggs and poultry are fine for most people.
- **Quantity isn't the priority for most adults.** The US 2025–30 target of 1.2–1.6 g/kg a day is an outlier. Older adults are the exception (see [Caveats](#caveats)).
- **Sources:** [IARC Q&A on red and processed meat](https://www.iarc.who.int/wp-content/uploads/2018/11/Monographs-QA_Vol114.pdf) · [Lescinsky et al., Nat Med 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9556326) · [Song et al., JAMA Intern Med 2016](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/27479196/) · [Naghshi et al., BMJ 2020](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7374797/) · [Cancer Council Australia](https://www.cancer.org.au/cancer-information/causes-and-prevention/diet-and-exercise/food-and-nutrition/meat-poultry-eggs-and-fish)

### Eat more legumes, nuts and seeds → Eat more legumes and nuts
- **Nuts have the strongest evidence of the three.**
  - About 28 g a day is linked with roughly 20% lower mortality, and most of the benefit comes by 15–20 g a day.
  - Trials show lower LDL cholesterol and no weight gain (Aune 2016; Arnesen 2023).
- **Legumes and seeds:** the evidence for legumes is moderate. Seeds have no separate outcome data, so they appear as an option rather than in the title.
- **Why it stays despite the overlap:** legumes, nuts and seeds also appear in other points, but this is the only one that gives an amount and a frequency. Low nut and seed intake ranks among the leading dietary risks worldwide (Global Burden of Disease 2017).
- **Sources:** [Aune et al., BMC Medicine 2016](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5137221/) · [Arnesen et al., Food Nutr Res 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC9930735/) · [Zargarzadeh et al., Adv Nutr 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10103007/)

### Drink more water → Make water your default drink
- **The strong evidence is for *replacing* sugary drinks.**
  - Each daily serving of a sugary drink is linked with about 13% higher diabetes risk (Imamura 2015).
  - Evidence for drinking extra water as such is weak (Hakam 2024), and the "8 glasses a day" rule has no evidence behind it.
- **Tea, coffee and plain milk hydrate too.** Canada's Food Guide uses this exact framing: "make water your drink of choice".
- **Juice counts as a sugary drink,** in line with the WHO, Canada and the NHS.
- **Diet drinks are left out of the wording.** Swapping sugary drinks for them helps a little with weight, but the WHO advises against relying on sweeteners.
- **Sources:** [Malik & Hu, Nat Rev Endocrinol 2022](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC8778490) · [Imamura et al., BMJ 2015](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4510779/) · [Canada's Food Guide](https://www.canada.ca/en/health-canada/services/food-guide/explore/healthy-eating-recommendations/make-water-drink-choice.html)

### Reduce added sugar → Sweeten with fruit and spice
- **Why reframed:** the point now leads with something to add, in line with the philosophy.
  - Fruit on unsweetened staples replaces added sugar and brings fibre and nutrients with it.
  - Spices and vanilla make food taste sweeter without adding sugar (see [Ways to sweeten](#ways-to-sweeten) below).
  - Starting from unsweetened cereal, yoghurt and porridge is the swap that makes this work, and it repeats daily.
- **Why it still matters:** the WHO limits *free* sugars to under 10% of energy, ideally under 5% (about 25 g a day). Australians average about 8%.
  - Free sugars include honey, syrups and juice, which is why honey and syrup are named. Drinks are covered by the water point.
  - Sugars in whole fruit don't count as free sugars, and worry about them is unfounded.
- **Where most added sugar comes from:** drinks and treats, not staples. In Australia the top sources are, in order (ABS 2023):
  - soft drinks (19.8%)
  - juice
  - sugar, honey and syrups
  - sweet biscuits
  - cakes and muffins

  For treats, the philosophy's "swaps rather than bans" applies. Reaching for fruit when you want something sweet is the easiest swap.
- **Australian nutrition panels show total sugars only,** so use the ingredient list to check a product is unsweetened.
- **Sources:** [WHO, Healthy diet fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/healthy-diet) · [ABS, Food and nutrients 2023](https://www.abs.gov.au/statistics/health/food-and-nutrition/food-and-nutrients/2023) · [Huang et al., BMJ 2023](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10074550)

#### Ways to sweeten

**Good options**
- **Sweet spices and vanilla** (cinnamon, nutmeg, cardamom, ginger):
  - Aromas raise perceived sweetness in taste tests. Vanilla made sweetened milk taste sweet enough that researchers estimated its sugar could be cut by 20–50%.
  - They work best alongside some sweetness, from fruit or milk.
  - Cinnamon's blood-sugar claims don't hold up. Cassia cinnamon contains coumarin, which is fine in food amounts.
- **Milk:**
  - Cooking porridge in milk adds gentle sweetness. Lactose isn't a free sugar.
  - Lactose-free milk tastes sweeter, with no sugar added.
  - If you use plant milks, choose unsweetened.
- **Dried fruit** (raisins, sultanas, dates, apricots):
  - Whole dried fruit isn't free sugar under the WHO or UK definitions, so it's a genuine swap.
  - It's concentrated. A heaped tablespoon of raisins (30 g) has about the sugar and kilojoules of a piece of fresh fruit, and the Australian guidelines count it as a fruit serve "only occasionally".
  - The NHS advises eating it with meals to protect teeth.
  - About a level tablespoon, 1–2 chopped dates or 2–3 apricot halves per bowl is plenty. Dried cranberries usually have added sugar.
- **Mashed banana or apple purée:**
  - Good in porridge, pancakes and baking, where it can replace some of the sugar.
  - The UK counts puréed fruit as free sugar. Date paste is the weakest option, since dates are about two-thirds sugar.
- **Roasting, toasting and warmth:** roasting vegetables, caramelising onions and toasting oats or nuts bring out sweetness, and warm food tastes sweeter than cold.

**No better than sugar**
- **Honey, maple syrup, agave, coconut sugar, rice malt syrup and date syrup:**
  - All count as free sugars, and none is meaningfully healthier.
  - The extra antioxidants from the richest of them amount to about one serve of berries.
  - If you use one, choose it for flavour, measure it and drizzle it on top.
- **Non-sugar sweeteners** (stevia, monk fruit, sucralose, aspartame):
  - They're approved by FSANZ and safe within acceptable daily intakes.
  - Replacing sugar with them gave about 1 kg more weight loss in trials.
  - The WHO conditionally advises against relying on them. They're fine as a temporary stepping stone.
  - Many stevia and monk-fruit blends are mostly erythritol, which can upset your gut and carries an unresolved question about blood clotting.

**Turning sweetness down:** cutting sugar makes a food taste sweeter within a few months (about 40% sweeter by month three in one trial). But overall liking for sweetness barely changes (Wise 2016; the industry co-funded Sweet Tooth Trial). Halve the added sweetness, then halve it again every week or two, one food at a time.

- **Sources:** [Swan et al. 2018, UK definition of free sugars](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5962881/) · [NHS, 5 A Day portion sizes](https://www.nhs.uk/live-well/eat-well/5-a-day/portion-sizes/) · [Penn State, vanilla and sweetness](https://www.psu.edu/news/research/story/vanilla-makes-milk-beverages-seem-sweeter) · [NCCIH, Cinnamon](https://www.nccih.nih.gov/health/cinnamon) · [Phillips et al. 2009](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/19103324/) · [WHO, non-sugar sweeteners guideline (2023)](https://www.who.int/news/item/15-05-2023-who-advises-not-to-use-non-sugar-sweeteners-for-weight-control-in-newly-released-guideline) · [FSANZ, Intense sweeteners](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/consumer/additives/sweeteners) · [Rogers & Appleton 2021](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/34855/) · [Witkowski et al., Nat Med 2023](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-023-02223-9) · [Wise et al. 2016](https://library.fabresearch.org/viewItem.php?id=10044) · [Sweet Tooth Trial](https://eprints.bournemouth.ac.uk/41626/)

### Moderate salt intake → Reduce salt
- **This is one of the strongest levers on the list.**
  - High sodium is a leading dietary risk worldwide.
  - Cutting it lowers blood pressure, and salt-substitute trials show fewer strokes and deaths.
  - "Where convenient" was weaker than every guideline.
  - Average Australian intake is about 3,600 mg of sodium a day, against a 2,000 mg target.
- **Most salt comes from packaged and takeaway food** (about 70–75%), not the salt shaker.
  - In Australia the top sources are mixed dishes based on bread, pasta or rice, then bread, processed meat and pastries.
  - So the point targets everyday staples, plus flavouring with herbs and spices.
- **Optional extra:**
  - The WHO (2025) suggests potassium-enriched salt substitutes for adults who use table salt. They are *not* for people with kidney disease or on medicines that affect potassium.
  - If you use salt, the WHO advises choosing iodised salt.
- **Sources:** [WHO, Salt reduction fact sheet](https://www.who.int/news-room/fact-sheets/detail/salt-reduction) · [WHO, Lower-sodium salt substitutes guideline (2025)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240105591) · [FSANZ, Salt](https://www.foodstandards.gov.au/consumer/nutrition/salthowmuch)

### Increase dietary variety → Vary your plant foods
- **Variety *within* healthy food groups is a consensus principle** (FAO/WHO, Australian guidelines, the UK's Eatwell Guide).
- **Variety for its own sake isn't supported.** More variety, treats included, makes people eat more (AHA 2018; Embling 2021). So the point now names plant foods only.
- **"30 plants a week"** comes from a self-selected citizen-science group. It was measured against gut-microbe diversity, not health outcomes, so it's fine as a personal challenge but not a principle.
- **Sources:** [FAO/WHO, What are healthy diets? (2024)](https://www.who.int/publications/i/item/9789240101876) · [Embling et al., AJCN 2021](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC7948867/) · [McDonald et al., mSystems 2018](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5954204/)

### Add rather than restrict → moved into the Overall Philosophy
- **Why it moved:** it describes *how* to change rather than *what* to eat, and the philosophy paragraph already said much the same. Read literally, it also clashed with the original points on cutting sugar and salt.
- **The idea holds up when framed as additions *and* swaps:**
  - Added foods help mainly when they displace others. Advice to eat more fruit and vegetables, with nothing else changed, had no effect on weight (Kaiser 2014).
  - Habit research favours "do" goals over "don't" goals: "it is not possible to form a habit for not doing something" (Gardner, Lally & Wardle 2012).
  - Rigid, all-or-nothing dieting is linked with more overeating than a flexible approach.
- **Sources:** [Kaiser et al., AJCN 2014](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4095660/) · [Gardner, Lally & Wardle, BJGP 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3505409/) · [Lally et al., EJSP 2010](https://doi.org/10.1002/ejsp.674)

## New point

### Include dairy or alternatives
- **Why add it:** dairy is the only core food group the original list left out. The Australian, NZ, UK, US (both editions) and Nordic guidelines all include it, and Canada's does in part.
- **Fat level is left open deliberately,** because guidelines disagree:
  - the 2013 Australian guidelines say "mostly reduced fat"
  - Australia's Heart Foundation lets healthy adults choose full- or reduced-fat milk, yoghurt and cheese
  - the US 2025–30 guidelines favour full fat

  Unflavoured versions avoid added sugar either way.
- **Sources:** [Heart Foundation, Dairy and heart health](https://www.heartfoundation.org.au/healthy-living/healthy-eating/dairy-and-heart-health) · [Australian Dietary Guidelines summary (FAO)](https://www.fao.org/nutrition/education/dietary-guidelines/regions/australia/en)

## Considered but not added

- **Drafted, then removed:** drink less alcohol, cook more often, read labels and eat mindfully. Guidelines widely endorse all four. Their reasoning and sources are in the previous version of this file (commit `f2bc6fb`).
- **Contested or weak evidence:**
  - avoiding seed oils, or cooking with butter or tallow
  - "eat more protein" or "protein at every meal"
  - always choosing full-fat, or always reduced-fat, dairy
  - "8 glasses of water a day"
  - "30 plants a week"
  - "never skip breakfast" (in trials, eating breakfast added about 260 kcal a day and didn't help weight loss)
  - intermittent fasting
  - switching to diet drinks, or avoiding all sweeteners
  - rules about specific additives
  - routine supplements for healthy adults
  - "a drink a day is good for the heart"
  - "everything in moderation"
  - low-carb or keto as a general rule
- **Rests on discredited research:** smaller plates and "don't shop hungry" both trace largely to Brian Wansink's retracted studies.
- **Better in the philosophy than as points:** forming habits, making the healthy option the easy one at home, and eating with others.
- **Out of scope:**
  - Food safety: the Australian, NZ and WHO guidelines include it, but it's about hygiene rather than diet quality.
  - Physical activity and weight.
  - Self-monitoring (food diaries, apps): the evidence comes from weight-loss programs, and it clashes with the no-perfectionism philosophy.

## Caveats

- **These are general principles for healthy adults.** Pregnancy, chronic disease (kidney disease, diabetes, heart disease), food allergies and eating disorders call for individual advice.
- **Older adults** may need more protein: at least 1.0–1.2 g/kg a day (PROT-AGE).
- **Guidelines are changing:**
  - The NHMRC expects to release a draft of the revised Australian Dietary Guidelines for public consultation in late 2026. Re-check this list when it appears.
  - The US 2025–30 guidelines depart from most other bodies, and from their own advisory committee, on protein amounts, full-fat dairy and butter or tallow. This list follows the majority view.

- The research agents shared a web-search allowance, which ran out partway through. After that they confirmed claims by opening primary sources directly.
- Some Australian government sites (NHMRC, Eat for Health) blocked automated access. Details about them come from search-result text or official mirrors (FAO, ABS).
- Every source linked here was opened or confirmed by a research agent.
